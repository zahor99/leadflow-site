1 · The new position

One sentence the whole page argues for:

We build AI systems inside your enterprise — your data never leaves your control, and every claim is demonstrated before you pay for the next stage.

This beats the current "Strategic AI Second" framing for paid traffic on three counts: it names the fear that makes CEOs hesitate (data leaving the building, GDPR exposure, employee data in prompts), it claims the identity buyers actually search for (a software development firm with enterprise AI experience — not a strategy house), and it is provable with the two references below. The current site's control-and-evidence DNA survives fully; it stops being philosophy and becomes the product.

Naming rules (non-negotiable): the flagship client is never named or made identifiable — contract confidentiality. The manufacturer reference stays anonymized in public copy exactly as on leadflowautomation.net ("multi-billion-dollar US electronics manufacturer") and is attributed to the Head of AI's prior delivery, not to CLH as an entity. Both stories below are written inside those rules; the exact anonymised wording should additionally get the flagship client's written sign-off before going live.

Problem — name the fear precisely

The AI your company already uses is the risk you haven't priced.

Shadow AI is already hereEmployees paste customer lists, contracts and colleagues' data into public chatbots because it works. Every paste is a disclosure you didn't authorise and can't retract.
GDPR doesn't pause for pilotsPersonal data in a prompt is processing under GDPR — employee data included. "The vendor said it's fine" is not a lawful basis, and the EU AI Act is adding obligations on top.
Tool vendors want your data on their sideMost AI products are priced on your data flowing through their infrastructure. The convenience is real. So is the dependency — and the exposure.

The answer isn't banning AI. It's building it where your data already lives — inside your tenant, behind your permissions, with personal data stripped before any model ever sees it.

Why: recognition section — articulates the problem better than the reader can. Each card is factual and checkable; no invented statistics. "Employee data" gets explicit billing per the new targeting. The pivot line sets up the architecture section as the resolution.

Architecture — privacy as a design decision (sharpened from current "Trust & Control")

Privacy is not a policy document. It's an architecture.

Your tenant, your accountsSystems run in your cloud environment on your agreements — models reached through your own enterprise endpoints, not our infrastructure. You can revoke our access any afternoon.
Personal data stops at the gateWhere personal data could reach a model, an anonymisation layer strips it first — tested against golden datasets before go-live, logged in production.
Least privilege, human approvalScoped read-only access by default. Consequential actions — a quote, an order, an outbound message — wait for a named person's sign-off.
Paper trail for your lawyerData-processing documentation, DPIA support and sub-processor transparency prepared as deliverables, not afterthoughts — aligned to GDPR and the EU AI Act as it lands.
Why: the current site's best material, recut from abstract principle to four concrete commitments a buyer can verify in a call. The fourth card is new — it converts the flagship engagement's legal groundwork (DPIA/DPA authoring, processing records) into a stated capability, which almost no dev shop offers.
